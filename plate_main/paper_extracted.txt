This paper investigates the modeling and aeroelastic properties of laminated plates with honeycomb core by a 
semi-analytical approach, where both acoustic load and general boundary conditions are considered. The 
equivalent material properties of the honeycomb structures are determined by the corrected Gibson ’s formula. 
The energy functional of the plate is derived based on the first-order shear deformation theory (FSDT) and the 
effect of aerodynamic pressure is taken into account by introducing the supersonic piston theory. A modified 
shear correction factor is adopted in this study to improve the accuracy of the method. The Hamilton ’s principle 
is employed to formulate the governing equations of the system, and the solutions of the problem with both 
classical and elastic boundary conditions are solved by a modified Ritz approach. The results obtained by the 
proposed method show good agreement with those by the finite element method and in open literature. Finally, 
parametric studies on the aeroelastic behaviors of the plate concerning the boundary conditions, layer thickness 
and honeycomb geometry are carried out.   
1.Introduction 
Sandwich plates with honeycomb core have been extensively used in 
aerospace, automobile, naval and many other fields where strength-to- 
weight ratio is a concern. It is known that structures in aerospace ap-
plications are usually exposed to severe aerodynamic and acoustic loads 
during cruise, which significantly affects their dynamic behaviors and 
may result in large structure response and panel flutter. When the flutter 
phenomenon occurs, the structure becomes unstable and keeps gath-
ering energy from the airflow, which may cause fatigue failure and 
finally leads to catastrophic consequence. Thus, it is highly desirable to 
investigate the aeroelastic characteristics of honeycomb plates. 
Panel flutter is a kind of self-excited vibration phenomenon where 
thin plates are subjected to supersonic flow directed parallel to their 
surfaces [1]. This phenomenon was first observed in 1940s [2] and 
gained much attention for causing structural failures in rockets and 
airplanes in the 1950s [3]. Since then, panel flutter has been extensively 
studied and a comprehensive review of the early studies on the panel 
flutter and aeroelastic stability of plates and shells was given by Dowell 
[4]. For panel flutter at supersonic speeds, both analytical and numerical 
methods are widely used to study its dynamic behaviors [5]. For several special cases, the exact closed-form solutions were derived analytically 
by Sun and Xing [6]. For more general cases, semi-analytical methods 
are preferred due to their higher flexibility. One of the well-known 
methods in flutter analysis is the assumed mode method (AMM). By 
adopting AMM, Barai and Durvasula [7] analyzed the flutter of a 3D 
hybrid laminated panel and found that aerodynamic damping is 
important in determining the aeroelastic stability boundaries. Song and 
Li [8,9] studied the aero-elastic and aero-thermo-elastic flutter behav -
iors of composite laminated plates in supersonic flow and corresponding 
control strategies were proposed. Later, they successfully investigated 
the flutter properties of plates with classical and elastic boundary con-
ditions using AMM [10]. However, it should be mentioned that despite 
its high convergence speed, the results may be unreliable for typical 
boundary conditions when using AMM [10]. To obtain more accurate 
results, the Ritz/Galerkin method is adopted to study the flutter char-
acteristics of plates. Dowell [11,12] used Galerkin method for investi -
gating the aero-elastic stability characteristics of a plate at supersonic 
speed, and he concluded that four to six modes should be used to obtain 
accurate results. Navazi and Haddadpour [13] studied the nonlinear 
aero-thermo-elastic behaviors of functionally graded plates in super -
sonic airflow. Shitov and Vedeneev [14] investigated the structural

moderately thick orthotropic plates. It should be mentioned that this 
method is not limited to a single rectangular plate, but can deal with 
more complex structures as well [21,22] . The finite element method 
(FEM) is also a powerful tool in panel flutter analysis [23–25]. The FEM 
was first introduced by Olson [26] to deal with panel flutter problems, 
and can be used for many types of structures such as rectangular plates 
[27,28] , curved plates [29–32] and stiffened plates [32–34]. By 
comparing with AMM, Song and Li [10] found that the results from the 
FEM are more precise than those of AMM. 
As can be seen from the aforementioned literature, although various 
numerical methods have been developed to deal with panel flutter, a 
reliable plate model is always necessary for modeling panel structures. A 
comprehensive review on the modeling of plate structures can be found 
in Refs. [35–37]. Modeling of plate structures is actually a three- 
dimensional (3D) problem. However, the 3D elastic solution for lami-
nated plates is usually very complex and difficult to obtain. To simplify 
the problem, researchers make some simplifications and reduce the 
problem from 3D to two-dimensional (2D) with reasonable accuracy. 
According to Carrera [38], the 2D assumptions of multilayered struc-
tures includes the Equivalent Single Layer (ESL) model and layer-wise 
(LW) model. According to the ESL theory, the displacement field of 
the plate is the expansion of its displacement parameters in the middle 
surface. Thus, the number of unknown variables is independent of the 
number of layers, which significantly simplifies the modeling and 
analysis of laminated plates. While in the LW theory, all layers are 
modeled as independent shell structures, thus the detailed response of 
individual layers can be obtained. Although adopting the LW theory 
usually leads to more accurate results, the solution procedure would 
become much more complex. Despite the differences between ESL and 
LW models, many researchers devoted themselves into finding unified 
solutions for laminated plates. The most well-known work is contributed 
by Carrera and known as Carrera Unified Formulation (CUF) [38,39] , 
which provides a simple and elegant form for laminated structures. It is 
worthwhile to mention that CUF applies to not only laminated panels, 
but also beams and shells. Interested readers may refer to Hu et al [40] 
for a review and Refs. [41–45] for the latest developments. 
For plates with honeycomb core, the honeycomb layer can be 
modeled as homogenous elastic solid with equivalent material proper -
ties. The most widely used approach for obtaining equivalent material 
properties of honeycomb structures was firstly introduced by Gibson and 
Ashby [46] and improved by Fu and Yin [47]. Since their method can be 
written in an analytical form, many researchers have adopted this 
approach in their studies [48–52]. Although this approach is widely 
accepted in many practical applications, it should be noted that the 
intricate microarchitecture-dependent mechanical characteristics of the 
honeycomb structures cannot be captured by this approach [53]. 
Recently, different higher-order continuum theories such as strain gradient theory [53,54] and micropolar theory [55–57] have been 
developed to accurately model the deformation of lattice structures. 
However, as the constitutive parameters can hardly be written in an 
analytical form, numerical procedure is still necessary for determining 
these parameters when higher-order continuum theories are adopted. 
To obtain a semi-analytical solution, the honeycomb core is modeled 
by equivalent material property in this paper. Thus, laminated plate 
theory can be adopted for the plate. There are mainly-three kinds of 
models widely used for laminated plates, namely, the classical plate 
theory (CPT), the first-order shell deformation theory (FSDT) and the 
higher-order shear deformation theory (HSDT). CPT is mainly used for 
plate structures when their thickness is considerably small compared 
with other dimensions. However, as CPT neglects the shear effect and 
rotary inertia, it becomes inaccurate when the thickness of the plate 
increases [58]. To overcome this limitation, FSDT is proposed and it has 
been proved to be accurate for moderately thick plates. Many re-
searchers try to use FSDT for studying the dynamics of honeycomb 
plates. For example, Park and Mongeaub [59] studied the vibration and 
sound radiation of a sandwich honeycomb panel using the FSDT. Duc et 
al [60–62] investigated the vibration and nonlinear response of hon-
eycomb shells with negative Poisson ’s ratio. However, in benchmark 
results given by Madhukar and Singha [63], the natural frequencies 
predicted by FSDT and HSDT agree well only when the plate is very thin. 
Yu and Cleghorn [50] compared the results of the free vibration of 
symmetric honeycomb panels by using CPT, FSDT and HSDT, and 
pointed out that both CPT and FSDT are not adequate for the flexural 
vibration analysis of honeycomb panels. In their study, the natural fre-
quencies of the honeycomb panel predicted by FSDT is much higher than 
those by HSDT unless the plate is extremely thin. Similar phenomenon is 
observed by Madhukar and Singha [64], which indicates FSDT cannot be 
directly used for moderately thick plates if accuracy is a concern. Thus, 
instead of FSDT, researches prefer HSDT when analyzing the dynamics 
of honeycomb plates. Zhang et al [65] used Reddy ’s third-order shear 
deformation theory to solve the free vibrations and nonlinear responses 
of a honeycomb sandwich plate. Kheirikhah and Akhavan-Rad [66] 
studied the free vibration of a composite-faced honeycomb plate using a 
high-order theory. Khare et al [67] presented a facet shell element based 
on higher-order theory for solving the dynamics of sandwich shells. Li 
and Zhu [68] introduced an iterative formulation based on Reddy ’s 
third-order plate theory and solved the free flexural vibration of multi -
layered honeycomb plates. Ansari et al [69] used HSDT along with von- 
Karman nonlinear kinematic relations to solve the nonlinear vibration of 
honeycomb plates with functionally graded carbon nanotube-reinforced 
composite face sheets. Nevertheless, although HSDT is more accurate for 
honeycomb plates, it leads to a much more complex formulation. 
The reason FSDT is not appropriate for honeycomb plates is that the 
FSDT does not satisfy the zero shear stress condition on the upper and 
lower surfaces of a plate. For isotropic plates, a constant shear correction 
factor 5/6 can be used to correct the shear stress. But for honeycomb 
plates, since the equivalent material properties of the honeycomb is 
quite soft compared with those of the face sheets, the FSDT would pre-
dict a much higher shear stress in the plate. Liu and Zhao [70] noticed 
that both FSDT and HSDT can obtain accurate results for honeycomb 
sandwich plates by simply neglecting the shear effects of the face sheets. 
Although their study is limited to sandwich plates where the shear 
modulus of face sheets is much higher than that of the core, and the 
thickness of the face sheets is much smaller than that of the core, it in-
dicates that FSDT can still be accurate for honeycomb plates if the shear 
effect is carefully treated. However, obtaining accurate results for gen-
eral honeycomb plates by FSDT still remains a challenge. 
In this paper, a semi-analytical solution for the dynamics of a hon-
eycomb plate is developed. The energy functional of the plate is derived 
based on FSDT and a modified shear correction factor is used to properly 
treat the shear effects. The aerodynamic pressure is modeled by the 
supersonic piston theory and the Hamilton ’s principle is employed to 
formulate the governing equations of the system. A modified Ritz Z. According to FSDT, the out-of-plane transverse shear stresses σ4 and 
σ5 are assumed to be constant throughout the thickness direction of the 
plate, which would lead to a decrease of accuracy. This problem is 
solved by multiplying the transverse stress by a shear correction factor. 
For homogeneous plate, the shear correction factor κ is typically taken as 
5/6 as given by Ref. [37,63] . Nevertheless, for typical sandwich struc-
tures with soft cores, directly using κ 5/6 results in a relatively large 
error [50,63,64] . This is due to the fact that considering the zero shear 
stress condition, the out-of-plane shear stresses in the face layers are 
actually small and letting κ 5/6 over estimates the shear effect, which 
finally results in a much higher natural frequency. To overcome the 
problem, a practical workaround is to neglect the shear effects of the 
face sheets when they are relatively thin [70]. Another solution to the 
problem is using a modified version of HSDT instead of FSDT to get more 
accurate solutions for plates with a honeycomb cores [68]. However, 
using HSDT leads to a much more complex formulation than using FSDT. 
To overcome the problem, a more accurate shear correction factor is 
introduced to eliminate the inaccuracy induced by the shear effect of the 
face layers. Thus, a simpler and more accurate solution to the vibration 
of the plate can be obtained by FSDT.

and the computational environment, it is usually acceptable to choose 
values between 1012 to 1015 for this kind of problem.

3.2.1. Stability and forced response 
The aeroelastic characteristics of the plate under aerodynamic load is 
studied first by solving its eigenvalue problem. It is known that the plate 
is stable when the real parts of all its eigenvalues are negative, and the 
plate becomes unstable when positive real part appears. Fig. 7(a) shows 
the real part of the eigenvalues. From the figure, it can be observed that 
the real parts of the eigenvalues are negative and almost constant and 
when λ is small. However, as λ keeps increasing and reaches the non- 
dimensional critical flutter aerodynamic pressure (CFAP) λcr 308.5, 
the real parts of its first two eigenvalues split into two branches, one of 
which goes down and the other one quickly increases and becomes 
positive, which implies the system becomes unstable. Fig. 7(b) shows the 
imaginary part of the eigenvalues, which is in proportion to its natural 
frequency by a factor of 2π. The figure shows that when λDλcr, the first 
natural frequency increases while the second natural frequency de-
creases, and they would coalesce near λcr. Thus, finding the intersection 
point of the natural frequencies provides another way for determining 
the flutter boundaries. The mode shapes of the plate without aero-
dynamic load and at CFAP are respectively shown in Fig. 8 and Fig. 9.It 
is obvious that the mode shapes change significantly when aerodynamic 
load is applied to the plate. The symmetry of the second and third mode 
shape of the plate, which is a result of the isotropy of the material 
property of the honeycomb layer, is also broken by the aerodynamic 
load. Another interesting finding is that the first two modes coincides 
when the aerodynamic pressure is equal to CFAP, and these two modes 
evolve from the first two modes of the plate without aerodynamic 
pressure. It should be kept in mind that although these two modes share 
the same mode shape, the stabilities of these two modes are different as 
explained above. 
The response of the plate under acoustic load is also studied. The 
RMS response of the plate under unit acoustic load is shown in Fig. 10. It 
should be mentioned that in this study, an additional structural damping 
η 0.01 is applied to the plate to ensure finite response. From the figure, 
it is obvious that the maximum response of the plate at CFAP is much 
larger than that without airflow. Another finding is that the symmetric 
modes do not contribute to the forced response of the plate at first, but 
they would appear when aerodynamic pressure is applied to the plate. 
This is because aerodynamic load would break the symmetry of the 
modes as discussed before.

3.2.4. Honeycomb geometry 
The influence of the honeycomb parameters on the dynamics of the 
plate is investigated. From Eq. (8), it can be found that the equivalent 
material parameters of the honeycomb core depend on the non- 
dimensional parameters η1, η2 and the cell angle θc. The influence of 
these parameters on the natural frequencies of the plate is shown in 
Fig. 20. It can be seen from the figure that with the increase of η1, the 
natural frequencies of the plate quickly increase at first. But when the 
value of η1 keeps increasing, the natural frequencies would go down. 
Another phenomenon is that when the value of η1 is far away from 1, the 
natural frequencies of the second and third modes are no longer coin-
cident. Since η1l2c/l1c denotes the ratio of hexagon edge length, the 
results show that changing the shape of the hexagons would break the 
isotropy of the honeycomb. The effect of non-dimensional cell wall 
thickness η2 on the free vibration of the plate is given in Fig. 20(b). 
Similarly, the natural frequencies of the plate firstly go up and then 
decrease. The natural frequencies of a structure depend on its mass and 
stiffness, larger wall thickness would increase the stiffness of the core 
layer, which leads to a higher natural frequency. However, when the 
wall thickness keeps increasing, more extra mass is added to the plate 
and finally lowers the natural frequencies of the plate. The influence of 
cell angle θc is also studied and shown in Fig. 20(c). From the figure, the 
natural frequencies of the honeycomb plate remain almost constant 
when θc is not vary large. However, the natural frequencies drop quickly 
after θc reaches 60•. 
To further study the effect of the honeycomb geometry parameters 
on the flutter behaviors of the plate, the variations of the CFAP are 
calculated and shown in Fig. 21. It can be seen from the figure that 
despite the changes of the natural frequencies, CFAP decreases with η1 
and increases with η2 monotonously. And with the increase of cell angle 
θc, CFAP decreases when θc is smaller that 10•or larger than 75•, and 
increases in other circumstances.

4.Conclusion 
In this paper, the modeling and aeroelastic properties of laminated 
plates with honeycomb core is investigated. By adopting the corrected 
Gibson ’s formula, the honeycomb layer is modeled by its equivalent 
material properties. Based on the FSDT, the energy functionals of the plate with general boundary conditions and acoustic load are formu -
lated, and the aerodynamic pressure is taken into consideration by using 
the supersonic piston theory. To ensure accuracy for plates with soft 
cores, a modified shear correction factor is adopted in this study to 
accurately model the out-of-plane shear stresses of the plate. The 
Hamilton ’s principle is employed to formulate the governing equations 
and the equations are solved by a modified Ritz approach. 
To show the reliability of the proposed method, a computer program 
is developed and the results are compared with those from FEM and 
other studies. A good agreement is observed from the results and the 
developed computer program shows to be much more efficient than the 
FEM. Finally, the influences of some key parameters of sandwich hon-
eycomb plates on the vibration and aeroelastic characteristics are 
analyzed. It is found that for sandwich plates with soft core, modified 
shear correction factor should be used to get accurate results if the plate 
has moderate thick face layers. Moreover, numerical studies show that 
proper modification of the honeycomb cell geometry may break the 
symmetry of the equivalent material properties and leads to changes of
